Why subtraction is worth doing

Enhancement is a change in T1 signal, not bright T1 signal. When a lesion is already bright before contrast, the post-contrast image cannot tell you whether it enhanced; the eye sees a bright lesion either way. Subtracting the pre removes the intrinsic signal and leaves only the gadolinium effect. Lesion H in the demo is the whole argument: on the post image it looks as bright as the enhancing lesion A, and on a clean subtraction it vanishes.

That is why subtraction earns its keep in blood-product and protein-rich lesions (hemorrhagic or proteinaceous renal and hepatic cysts, endometriomas, hemorrhagic adnexal lesions), in T1-bright hepatic observations, after ablation or embolization where the treated zone is often T1-bright, and in Bosniak v2019 assessment of cystic renal masses. Its value collapses the moment its core assumption fails: the same voxel must hold the same tissue, measured the same way, on both acquisitions.

What goes wrong when pre and post don't match

Breath-hold mismatch

The most common failure in the abdomen. Liver, spleen and kidneys move with the diaphragm while the body wall and spine stay put, and different organs move by different amounts. That makes it non-rigid, so a single rigid registration cannot fix it.

Signature: paired bright and dark bands on opposite sides of structures, all oriented the same way. Worst at high-contrast interfaces: T1-bright lesion against liver, liver dome against lung, kidney poles against fat.

Pseudo-enhancement of T1-bright lesions

Shift a hemorrhagic cyst by a few pixels and the leading edge becomes a bright crescent on a clinical-window subtraction. It looks like a thin enhancing wall or a mural nodule, which can upgrade a Bosniak category or suggest viable tumor after treatment.

Signature: crescent on one side only; the opposite side goes negative (hidden as black in the clinical window).

Masked true enhancement

The same bands can cancel real enhancement at an edge, and through-plane motion means you may be subtracting a slice that does not contain the lesion at all. Small arterial-phase foci and thin septa are most at risk.

Signature: vessel positions and the liver dome level differ between pre and post on the same slice number.

Scaling / receiver gain mismatch

If the post series is scaled differently (separate prescan or auto-scaling, different rescale slope, a changed coil selection), every pixel is multiplied by a factor. Non-enhancing tissue that is bright on the pre leaves a proportional residual.

Signature: global, not edge-based. Bright non-enhancing structures (H, marrow, blood products) "enhance" uniformly; dark ones barely change.

Inconsistent fat suppression

Fat saturation that works on one acquisition and fails on the other, often at the periphery of the field of view or near metal, leaves fat on the subtraction. In the pelvis and at the body wall this mimics enhancement of fat-containing or marginal lesions.

Signature: subcutaneous and mesenteric fat not black; often patchy and worst at the edges of the field.

Registered subtraction side effects

Vendor or PACS non-rigid registration reduces band artifacts but interpolates and warps. It can blur a thin wall, shrink a small nodule, or deform the region you care about. Know whether the images in front of you were registered.

Signature: unusually smooth subtraction with soft edges; lesion size or shape differs from the source images.

How to QA a subtraction map

  1. Know what you are looking at. Which post phase minus which pre, generated on the scanner or in PACS, registered or not. Pre and post should share sequence parameters (TR, TE, flip angle, FOV, matrix, slice positions, fat suppression, coil selection) and the same signal scaling, for example by keeping prescan and receiver gain fixed between the two acquisitions. If the scaling is uncertain, check the source series in the DICOM header.
  2. Survey the whole slice before the lesion. Background, subcutaneous fat, marrow and muscle should be near-black on the clinical window, with muscle only faint. Bright fat points to fat-sat inconsistency; a uniform residual in bright non-enhancing tissue points to scaling.
  3. Check an internal negative control near the lesion. Gallbladder bile, a simple cyst, CSF, or urine in the collecting system before excretion should null. If the control does not null, the subtraction cannot prove or exclude enhancement at that level.
  4. Hunt for edge signatures. Look for paired bright and dark bands that point the same way across different organs. Switch to a signed or gray-centered window if your viewer allows it; negative bands are hidden on the default window. Compare liver dome level and vessel positions on the pre and post at the same slice.
  5. Ask whether the "enhancement" follows the lesion or the motion. True enhancement respects lesion morphology: a nodule, a thick septum, the whole solid component. Artifact sits on the edge that faces the direction of motion and shows up on every high-contrast interface in the same direction.
  6. Scroll adjacent slices and other phases. Real enhancement is reproducible across slices and follows an expected time course across arterial, portal venous and delayed phases. Artifact changes with each breath-hold.
  7. Measure on the source images when in doubt. Place matched ROIs on pre and post (copy-paste the ROI, adjusting only for obvious anatomic shift) and compute the percent change, compared against a reference non-enhancing structure at the same level. Treat any published threshold as supporting evidence rather than a verdict, especially in small or heterogeneous lesions.
  8. Report the limitation. If misregistration makes the subtraction unreliable, say so and state what your enhancement call rests on instead: source images, quantitative ROIs, DWI, or a recommendation for repeat imaging, CT or contrast-enhanced ultrasound.

Useful report phrasing:

Subtraction images are degraded by respiratory misregistration at this level; the apparent peripheral enhancement of the right renal lesion is attributed to artifact. On source images, there is no measurable enhancement (signal change <5% relative to pre-contrast).

Things to try with the resident

Set a 4-pixel craniocaudal shift in the clinical window and ask what H shows. Then switch to the signed window and let them find the matching dark crescent. Add bulk motion, turn on rigid registration, and show that the body wall cleans up while the liver bands stay. Finally, set scaling to 1.12 with no motion: there are no edge artifacts at all, yet H and the marrow now "enhance". That case is the one most likely to fool someone who only looks for misregistration bands.
