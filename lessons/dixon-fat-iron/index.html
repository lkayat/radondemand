Why fat and water go in and out of phase

Fat protons (mostly methylene, CH2) resonate about 3.4 ppm below water. That is roughly 217 Hz at 1.5 T and 434 Hz at 3 T, so after excitation the fat signal rotates relative to water, completing a full turn every 4.6 ms at 1.5 T and every 2.3 ms at 3 T. Sample at half a turn and the two cancel (opposed phase); sample at a full turn and they add (in phase). A voxel containing both fat and water therefore loses signal on the opposed-phase image. That is the whole basis of chemical-shift imaging, and the phase clock above shows it directly.

Real fat is not one peak. It has at least six, so fat never perfectly realigns with water, and the in-phase time based on the main peak is only approximately in phase. Separately, every echo is later than the one before, so T2* decay removes signal no matter what fat is doing. Those two facts drive most of the pitfalls on this page.

Reading the dual-echo pair

Microscopic (intravoxel) fat

Fat and water mixed within the same voxel: lipid-laden cells in steatosis, lipid-rich adrenal adenoma, steatotic hepatocellular adenoma, some HCCs, and clear cell RCC and its metastases. The whole lesion drops in signal on opposed phase, and fat saturation does little because fat is a minority of the voxel.

Try: adrenal adenoma case. Quantify with signal intensity index or adrenal-to-spleen ratio, never liver as reference if the liver may be steatotic.

Macroscopic fat

Voxels that are mostly fat: angiomyolipoma, myelolipoma, teratoma, lipoma, liposarcoma. The fat follows subcutaneous fat on every sequence and drops on fat-saturated images. It stays bright on opposed phase because a fat-dominant voxel has almost no water to cancel against.

Signature: an India-ink (etching) black line on opposed phase at the fat–tissue interface, where boundary voxels hold both.

Chemical shift of the first kind

Separate from phase cancellation: because fat resonates at a different frequency, it is mapped to the wrong position along the frequency-encoding direction. It appears as a bright band on one side of a fat interface and a dark band on the other, on every echo.

Try: drop pixel bandwidth to about 150 Hz and watch the AML and perirenal fat bands, then swap frequency direction.

Susceptibility and the later echo

Metal, gas, hemosiderin, calcification and parenchymal iron create local field gradients. Spins dephase within the voxel, and dephasing accumulates with time. The later echo therefore shows more signal loss and blooming. At 1.5 T with the usual echo order, that later echo is the in-phase one.

Rule: drop on opposed phase suggests fat; drop on the later echo (usually in-phase) suggests T2* from iron or susceptibility.

The echo-order trap

Some 3 T protocols acquire in-phase at about 2.3 ms and opposed phase at about 3.5 ms. The opposed-phase image is then both the fat-cancelling echo and the more T2*-weighted one, so iron or susceptibility produces an opposed-phase drop that mimics microscopic fat.

Always read the TEs before calling fat. Try the 3 T pitfall case.

Recognising which image is which

Series labels are not always trustworthy. The opposed-phase image shows India ink around the kidneys, spleen and bowel. The in-phase image shows more blooming around bowel gas, clips and pneumobilia when it is the later echo.

Check the TE in the DICOM header when something does not fit.

From dual echo to quantification

A two-point magnitude estimate, signal fat fraction = (IP − OP) / (2 × IP), is quick but confounded in five ways: T2* decay between echoes (iron or susceptibility biases it down, or up if OP is later); T1 weighting, since fat recovers faster than water at typical flip angles and is over-represented; the multi-peak fat spectrum; noise bias in low-signal voxels; and a hard ceiling at 50%, because magnitude data cannot tell whether fat or water is dominant. A 90% fat voxel reads as 10%.

Chemical shift–encoded multi-echo methods remove these confounders. Siemens q-Dixon (LiverLab) and GE IDEAL-IQ, like Philips mDIXON Quant, acquire about six echoes of a 3D spoiled gradient echo with a low flip angle (about 3–5°) to minimise T1 bias. They fit each voxel with a multi-peak fat model plus a common R2* term, using complex or hybrid fitting that resolves fat-dominant from water-dominant voxels. Outputs are water, fat, proton density fat fraction (PDFF) and R2* maps, with field or quality maps depending on the vendor. PDFF is a reproducible biomarker across vendors and field strengths.

In the simulator, the multi-echo panels use exactly this idea: six-peak fat model, single R2*, real-valued water and fat fitted to the complex echoes. Raise the quantitative flip angle to see T1 bias, stretch the echo spacing in severe iron at 3 T to see the fit fail, or put a voxel over the AML to see the 50% ceiling of the dual-echo estimate.

How to QA a PDFF / R2* exam

  1. Confirm the protocol. Multi-echo (usually six) with low flip angle and multi-peak modeling, not a two-point Dixon. Note the field strength, because R2* values and iron calibrations depend on it.
  2. Check internal controls. Spleen PDFF should be near 0%, subcutaneous fat should read about 90% or more, and spleen R2* should be low unless there is reticuloendothelial iron. If these fail, distrust the liver number.
  3. Look for fat–water swaps. A region where subcutaneous fat reads as water, or liver PDFF jumps above 50%, usually at the edge of the field of view or near gas. Scroll the water and fat images, not just the PDFF map.
  4. Read the R2* map before placing ROIs. Blooming from bowel gas, the lung base, clips or pneumobilia raises R2* and disturbs the fit nearby. Keep ROIs away from these, from vessels, bile ducts and the liver edge.
  5. Sample widely. Use several large ROIs (for example one per lobe or segment group, or whole-liver segmentation) and place identical ROIs on PDFF and R2*. Heterogeneous steatosis and focal sparing are common.
  6. Respect the measurable range. When R2* is very high the signal has largely gone by the first echo, so R2* is underestimated and PDFF is unreliable. Report it as exceeding the method's range and consider 1.5 T with shorter echoes, or R2-based methods.
  7. Correlate with dual echo and T2-weighted images. Does liver signal drop on the later echo? Is the spleen or marrow dark? Does the pattern fit a clinical story? Discordance usually means an artifact or a confounder.
  8. Report what you measured and how. PDFF in %, R2* in s⁻¹ with field strength, ROI method, and any estimated LIC with the calibration used.
