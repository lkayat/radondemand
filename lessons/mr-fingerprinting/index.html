MR Fingerprinting: acquire, simulate, match, map

Conventional relaxometry measures one property at a time and fits a curve to a handful of points. MRF deliberately varies the acquisition so that every tissue traces its own signal evolution, then identifies the tissue by comparing that evolution against a simulated dictionary. This page runs the whole chain on a synthetic prostate slice, using a FISP-MRF schedule modeled on the prostate protocol, with a real extended-phase-graph simulator, SVD compression and dictionary matching running in your browser.

1Acquisition: making tissues sing different songs

An inversion pulse is followed by hundreds of TRs, each with its own flip angle and TR. After every excitation a single spiral interleaf is read, so each time frame is heavily undersampled and full of aliasing. No individual frame is a usable image, but every voxel's signal over time carries its tissue's signature.

3Pattern matching: finding each voxel's closest fingerprint

Each voxel's measured time course is normalized and compared with every normalized dictionary entry by inner product. The entry with the highest correlation gives T1 and T2; the scale factor between measurement and entry gives M0. Aliasing and noise differ from frame to frame, so they are nearly orthogonal to every fingerprint and barely move the peak.

4Maps and post-processing

One acquisition yields perfectly co-registered T1, T2 and M0 maps, a match-quality map, and any synthetic contrast you care to compute from them. B1 inhomogeneity at 3 T biases the match unless the dictionary includes B1 and a B1 map (for example Bloch–Siegert, as in the abdominal MRF work) selects the right slice.

5Why MRF is more precise than curve fitting

Conventional T1 mapping acquires a few inversion times and fits S = a + b·e−TI/T1; conventional T2 mapping acquires a few spin echoes and fits S = A·e−TE/T2. Each point is a full image, each property needs its own scan, and every error in each point goes straight into a three- or two-parameter fit. MRF instead constrains T1, T2 and M0 jointly with hundreds of samples, and matching against a physically simulated dictionary means effects such as stimulated echoes and imperfect B1 are modeled rather than ignored. The experiment below repeats both approaches hundreds of times on the same voxel with fresh noise, so you can see the spread of estimates directly. The fair comparison is at equal scan time: reference inversion-recovery and spin-echo mapping take many minutes per slice, while one MRF pass takes seconds. Precision per minute of scanning, plus the absence of misregistration between separately acquired series, is where the reproducibility advantage comes from. Because matching snaps to dictionary entries, use the fine grid in section 2 for the most faithful spread.

Monte Carlo test–retest

Noise model: every image needs 48 readouts (spiral interleaves or turbo shots). A reference inversion-recovery image uses TR 10 s, a multi-echo spin-echo series TR 2 s, and each MRF frame is a single interleaf, so it carries √48 ≈ 7× the noise of a fully sampled image. At equal scan time the MRF acquisition can be repeated and averaged. Aliasing is left out here. The spin-echo train is simulated with stimulated echoes, so B1 errors bias its T2.

6What the evidence shows

Repeatability in a standard phantom

< 2% / < 4.7%

Within-scanner coefficient of variation for T1 and T2 over repeated prostate FISP-MRF measurements of the ISMRM/NIST system phantom, across T1 of roughly 300–2400 ms and T2 of roughly 19–250 ms.

Lo et al., 2022

Reproducibility across continents

< 4.9% / < 8.1%

Between-scanner variation for T1 and T2 on five 3 T scanners at three institutions in the United States and Brazil, all running the same online reconstruction; in vivo prostate test–retest ICC exceeded 0.92.

Lo et al., 2022

Prostate cancer versus normal PZ

1628 vs 2247 ms

Mean T1 of cancer versus normal-appearing peripheral zone; T2 was 73 versus 169 ms. Combined with ADC, MRF separated cancer from normal PZ and helped grade lesions in later work.

Yu et al., 2017; Panda et al., 2019

Where MRF is still fragile

Very short T2 (below about 30 ms) varies more, partly from coarse dictionary steps; very long T2 can be underestimated when the dictionary range is capped. Motion, partial volume, B0 drift and B1 all add variation, which is why B1-insensitive designs and B1 correction matter.

Lo et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2016

7QA checklist for MRF maps

Check the dictionary. Know its T1 and T2 ranges and step sizes. Values piling up at the edge of the range, or posterized maps, point to range or resolution limits rather than biology.

Check B1 handling. At 3 T in the pelvis and abdomen, confirm whether B1 was measured and included in matching, or whether a B1-insensitive schedule was used. Left–right gradients in T2 across uniform tissue suggest uncorrected B1.

Look at the match-quality map. Low correlation flags motion, partial volume, flow, off-resonance or tissue behavior the dictionary does not model.

Place ROIs away from boundaries. MRF voxels at tissue interfaces match a single compromise entry; small lesions and thin structures are the most affected. Keep the same ROI method across time points.

Compare like with like. MRF values are reproducible for a given sequence and reconstruction, but are not interchangeable with values from other methods or schedules. Use protocol-specific reference ranges, and phantom QA to monitor drift.

Teaching simulation. The FISP schedule (flip angles about 3–50°, TR about 11–14 ms, inversion-prepared) is modeled on published prostate MRF parameters, but the lobe shapes, TR pattern, dictionary grids, aliasing model and tissue values are illustrative. The EPG simulation uses a truncated number of dephasing states, signals are real-valued, and there is no slice-profile, off-resonance, diffusion, magnetization transfer or motion modeling. Numbers produced here demonstrate principles and should not be quoted as measurements. For teaching only; not for clinical use.
