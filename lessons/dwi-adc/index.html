Diffusion-weighted imaging: from b-values to ADC to calculated b

Every tissue on this page decays with its own mix of perfusion, diffusion and T2, and is imaged at whatever b-values you choose. Watch the curves separate, flip the plot to a log scale to see them straighten into lines whose slopes are the ADC, then extrapolate those lines to build a calculated high b-value.

What the curves are telling you

Vessels: gone by b 50

Flowing blood and capillary perfusion move so fast that even small diffusion gradients dephase them. Signal collapses at the lowest b-values, which is why b 50 images look black-blood.

This fast component is the IVIM perfusion fraction. It inflates any ADC that includes b 0.

Water: bright start, steep fall

Cysts and bile have long T2, so they start bright on b 0. Water diffuses freely, so the log-line is steep (ADC about 3.0). At b 800 a cyst may still be faintly visible; at b 1400 it is gone.

Some residual brightness at high b in a fluid structure is T2 shine-through, not restriction.

Parenchyma: shallow slopes

Liver, spleen, pancreas and prostate zones decay more slowly because cells and membranes hinder water. The spleen and normal lymph nodes are naturally restricted and bright on high b, a useful internal reference.

Liver has a large perfusion fraction, so its ADC depends heavily on the lowest b-value used.

Tumor: the line that refuses to fall

High cellularity restricts diffusion, so tumor keeps its signal at high b while everything around it fades. That is the whole contrast mechanism of DWI: the tumor is not getting brighter, the background is getting darker.

The flattest line on the log plot is the lowest ADC.

Why the log transform matters

On a linear plot, a mono-exponential curve bends everywhere, and its tangent gets shallower as the signal falls, so the slope says nothing directly about diffusion. Take the natural log and the same curve becomes a straight line: ln S = ln S₀ − ADC·b. The slope is −ADC, the intercept is ln S₀, and the intercept carries all the T2 weighting. Two tissues with the same slope have the same ADC regardless of how bright they started. That is why ADC removes T2 shine-through and why high b images alone cannot.

Real tissue is not perfectly mono-exponential. At low b, the perfusion component makes the log curve start steeper (IVIM). At high b, restricted and hindered compartments make it curve upward (non-Gaussian behavior, described by kurtosis). At very high b, noise adds a floor that flattens the curve further. Turn on tangents and drag the cursor: in log view each tangent slope is the local ADC. A pure mono-exponential tissue has the same local ADC everywhere; real tissue does not, which is why ADC values depend on which b-values you choose, and why protocols must stay consistent for follow-up.

Calculated (extrapolated) b-values

PI-RADS v2.1 asks for a high b-value image of at least 1400 s/mm², read alongside the ADC map. Acquiring b 1400 or 2000 directly needs stronger or longer diffusion gradients, which means a longer TE, lower signal-to-noise and more distortion, and at the highest b the signal of normal tissue approaches the noise floor. The alternative is to compute it. Having fitted each voxel to ln S = ln S₀ − ADC·b from the acquired b-values (typically b 50–100 and 800–1000), the scanner or workstation simply evaluates the line further out:

S(bcalc) = S₀,fit · e−ADC·bcalc

Advantages

No extra scan time. No longer TE, so no extra T2 decay or distortion. No Rician noise floor, so background prostate is properly dark. Perfect registration with the ADC map, and any b-value can be generated after the fact.

What it inherits

Everything in the source images: their noise (amplified, because an error in ADC is multiplied by b), their artifacts and distortion, and their T2 weighting, so shine-through persists. Including b 0 adds perfusion bias that darkens tissue on the calculated image.

What it assumes

That the tissue stays mono-exponential beyond the highest acquired b. Real tissue, especially tumor, shows non-Gaussian diffusion, so its true signal at b 2000 is higher than the extrapolation. Turn on "Extrapolate" and compare the dashed line with the true curve and the acquired point.

How to QA a DWI series

  1. Confirm the b-values. Check which b-values were acquired, which built the ADC map (the DICOM header or series description), and whether the high b is acquired or calculated. Numbers from different b sets are not comparable.
  2. Look at the low b image first. It is your anatomic and artifact check: distortion near rectal gas, bowel gas or metal; ghosting; fat suppression failure; motion. Anything wrong here is baked into the ADC and the calculated b.
  3. Always pair high b with ADC. Bright on high b and dark on ADC is restriction. Bright on both is shine-through. Dark on both is T2 blackout. Never call restriction on the high b alone.
  4. Check the noise floor. On acquired high b images, look at the background and at tissue that should be dark. If normal tissue is not darker than the noise, low ADC values in that region may just be the floor.
  5. Use internal references. Spleen and lymph nodes are restricted; bile, CSF and urine should have ADC near 3.0; normal PZ usually above 1.4. If references are off, suspect the fit, b-value choice or artifacts.
  6. Place ROIs carefully. Stay inside the lesion, away from edges, cyst walls, vessels and susceptibility. Report the b-values used and whether the value is a mean or minimum.
  7. Scrutinize calculated high b images. Compare with an acquired high b if you have one. Bright foci that exist only on the calculated image, or that sit where the source images show artifact, deserve suspicion.
  8. Correlate with T2 and DCE. In the PZ, DWI is dominant; in the TZ, T2 is. A DWI finding without a T2 correlate, or a markedly restricted TZ nodule that is well circumscribed and encapsulated on T2, should be scored with the zone rules in mind.
