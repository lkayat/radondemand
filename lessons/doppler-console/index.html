Carotid criteria (SRU 2003 consensus)

Grade the ICA with the peak systolic velocity and the gray-scale and color estimate of plaque; ICA/CCA PSV ratio and ICA EDV are secondary. Measure the highest velocity in the stenosis with the angle cursor parallel to the jet, θ ≤ 60°, and the CCA PSV 2–3 cm below the bulb.

Normal: PSV < 125 cm/s, no plaque, ratio < 2.0, EDV < 40.
< 50%: PSV < 125, plaque < 50%, ratio < 2.0, EDV < 40.
50–69%: PSV 125–230, plaque ≥ 50%, ratio 2.0–4.0, EDV 40–100.
≥ 70% (not near-occlusion): PSV > 230, ratio > 4.0, EDV > 100.
Near-occlusion: markedly narrowed lumen (string sign); velocities high, low or undetectable.
Occlusion: no detectable patent lumen, no flow.

Grant EG, et al. Carotid artery stenosis: gray-scale and Doppler US diagnosis. Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound consensus conference. Radiology. 2003;229:340–346.

Aorta and mesenteric reference points

AAA: infrarenal aorta ≥ 3.0 cm (or ≥ 1.5 × normal). Measure outer wall to outer wall, perpendicular to the aortic axis, including mural thrombus; elective repair is usually considered at 5.5 cm in men and 5.0 cm in women, or with rapid growth. A common iliac artery aneurysm is generally ≥ 1.8–2.0 cm.

Mesenteric: fasting SMA is high resistance with early diastolic reversal; after a meal it becomes low resistance. The celiac trunk is low resistance at all times. Velocity thresholds often cited for ≥ 70% stenosis: SMA PSV > 275 cm/s, celiac PSV > 200 cm/s. Median arcuate ligament compression raises celiac velocity in expiration, with improvement in inspiration.

After EVAR: the excluded sac should be free of flow and stable or shrinking. Type I leaks arise at attachment sites, type II from branch backflow (lumbar or inferior mesenteric arteries, often to-and-fro), type III from graft defects or junctions, type IV from graft porosity, and type V is sac growth without visible leak (endotension).

Dissection: an intimal flap divides a true lumen (usually smaller, expanding in systole, with brisk forward systolic flow) from a false lumen (larger, slower, delayed or reversed flow, may thrombose).

Renal transplant reference points

Intrarenal resistive index (interlobar or segmental arteries, upper, mid and lower poles): RI = (PSV − EDV) / PSV, normally about 0.6–0.8. RI above 0.8 is abnormal but non-specific: acute rejection, acute tubular necrosis, calcineurin-inhibitor toxicity, obstruction, perinephric compression, renal vein thrombosis, and excessive probe pressure all raise it. Trend it against prior studies.

Transplant renal artery stenosis: anastomotic PSV > 250 cm/s (some centers use > 300), renal artery to external iliac PSV ratio > 1.8 (some use > 3), post-stenotic turbulence, and intrarenal tardus–parvus (acceleration time > 0.1 s, acceleration < 300 cm/s²). A kink can raise velocity focally with normal intrarenal waveforms. With two renal arteries, each anastomosis and the pole it supplies must be interrogated: a stenosis may show tardus–parvus in only one pole.

Veins: renal vein thrombosis gives absent venous flow, an enlarged graft and reversed, plateau-like diastolic flow in intrarenal arteries. Renal vein stenosis: velocity ratio > 3 across the anastomosis. Post-biopsy AV fistula: high-velocity low-resistance feeding artery, perivascular color vibration, arterialized draining vein. Segmental infarct: wedge-shaped perfusion defect on sensitive power Doppler.

Lower extremity arterial reference points

Waveforms: normal resting limb arteries are triphasic or biphasic (high resistance). Monophasic flow means either low distal resistance (hyperemia: brisk upstroke, short acceleration time) or upstream disease (tardus–parvus: slow upstroke, prolonged acceleration time, low rounded peak).

Stenosis: grade by the peak systolic velocity ratio (PSV in the stenosis ÷ PSV 1–4 cm upstream in a normal segment). Commonly used: ratio ≥ 2.0 for ≥ 50% and ≥ 4.0 for about ≥ 75% stenosis. Above an occlusion expect a high-resistance "thump"; below it, monophasic reconstituted flow fed by collaterals.

Popliteal aneurysm: diameter ≥ 1.5 times the adjacent normal artery (often > 1.5 cm); repair is usually considered at about 2 cm or with thrombus. About half are bilateral, and many patients also have an AAA. Pseudoaneurysm: yin-yang sac flow with to-and-fro flow in the neck. AV fistula: low-resistance high-velocity feeding artery, arterialized pulsatile vein, perivascular tissue vibration.

Lower extremity venous reference points

Compression: in transverse, apply enough pressure to deform the adjacent artery slightly; a normal vein coapts completely. Non-compressibility is the primary sign of DVT. Acute thrombus is hypoechoic and distends the vein; chronic post-thrombotic change is echogenic, the vein is small with thick walls, synechiae and often reflux.

Doppler: normal CFV flow is spontaneous and phasic with respiration, augments with calf squeeze and stops with Valsalva. Continuous, non-phasic CFV flow suggests a more proximal (iliac or IVC) obstruction, especially if one-sided; pulsatile CFV flow suggests elevated right heart pressure or tricuspid regurgitation.

Reflux (SVS/AVF 2011): abnormal if > 0.5 s in superficial, deep femoral and calf veins, > 1.0 s in the femoral and popliteal veins (and > 0.35 s in perforators). Superficial thrombophlebitis within 3 cm of the saphenofemoral junction is generally managed like DVT. A Baker cyst lies between the medial gastrocnemius and semimembranosus tendons and has no flow.

Quick quiz

Concepts behind the knobs

The Doppler equation

The frequency shift is fD = 2 f0 v cos θ / c. The scanner measures fD and solves for v using the angle you give it. Get the angle wrong and every velocity is wrong; at 90° there is no shift at all, which is why a vessel parallel to a flat linear probe can look empty on color.

PRF, Nyquist and depth

Pulsed Doppler samples the shift once per pulse, so it can only measure up to PRF / 2 (the Nyquist limit) before wrapping around: aliasing. The next pulse cannot leave until the last echo returns, so PRFmax ≈ c / (2 × depth). Lower transmit frequency, baseline shift, a smaller angle or HPRF are the escape routes.

Sample volume and spectral broadening

A small gate in the center of a laminar vessel samples a narrow band of velocities and leaves a clear window under the envelope. A large gate, or one near the wall, collects slow edge flow and wall motion, filling the window and mimicking turbulence.

Wall filter

Strong, slow echoes from walls and tissue swamp the display near the baseline. The wall filter removes shifts below a cutoff. Too low leaves thumps; too high deletes real slow flow such as diastolic or venous flow.

Color versus spectral versus power

Color Doppler shows the mean shift in each pixel, uncorrected for angle, at a frame rate limited by box size, depth and ensemble length. Spectral Doppler shows the full velocity distribution at one location and can be angle-corrected. Power Doppler shows signal strength only: no direction, no aliasing, more sensitivity to slow flow.

Aliasing or true reversal?

Aliasing jumps across the brightest colors (yellow next to cyan) because the velocity exceeded the scale. True reversal passes through black, through zero velocity. On the spectrum, aliasing wraps the peak to the opposite side; a reversed component starts at the baseline.
