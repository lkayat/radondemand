Three ways to grade the same strain

ACR–SAR–SPR consensus (PCL)

The pubococcygeal line runs from the inferior margin of the pubic symphysis to the last coccygeal joint. Measure the bladder base, cervix or vaginal vault, and the peritoneal sac perpendicular to it at maximal descent, usually during evacuation. Rule of three: below the PCL by < 3 cm mild, 3–6 cm moderate, > 6 cm severe. Anorectal junction descent > 3 cm below the PCL indicates excessive pelvic floor descent. Rectocele depth: < 2 cm small, 2–4 cm moderate, > 4 cm large.

HMO system (Comiter)

Three points: A, the inferior margin of the pubic symphysis; B, the posterior aspect of the puborectalis sling; C, the junction of the first and second coccygeal elements. The PCL runs from A to C. The H line (A to B) is the anteroposterior hiatal width (normal ≤ 6 cm); the M line is the shortest distance from B to the PCL and measures pelvic floor descent (normal ≤ 2 cm). Relaxation: H 6–8 / M 2–4 mild, 8–10 / 4–6 moderate, ≥ 10 / ≥ 6 severe. Organs are graded by how far they pass below the H line: 0–2 cm grade 1, 2–4 cm grade 2, > 4 cm grade 3.

Mid-pubic line (Singh)

A line along the long axis of the pubic symphysis, extended caudally, approximates the hymenal plane used in clinical POP-Q staging. Distances perpendicular to it can be mapped onto POP-Q-like stages: more than 1 cm above (stage 1), within 1 cm (stage 2), more than 1 cm below (stage 3), complete eversion (stage 4). Useful when talking to urogynecologists.

The three systems use different reference lines, so the same organ can be "mild" in one and "moderate" in another. State which system you used in every report.

Structured report

Generated from the current case at maximal descent, in the style of the ACR–SAR–SPR consensus template. The staging statements follow the grading system selected above.

Protocol and patient preparation

Indications

Obstructed defecation, fecal incontinence, pelvic organ prolapse (especially multicompartment or recurrent after repair), pelvic pain or pressure, and pre-operative planning where clinical examination underestimates the posterior or peritoneal compartments.

Preparation

Explain and rehearse each maneuver; coaching drives the result. Fill the rectum with about 120–180 mL of ultrasound gel (optionally mixed with gadolinium for T1 imaging); vaginal gel (about 20 mL) helps outline the vagina. A moderately filled bladder: an empty bladder hides cystoceles, a very full one can mask posterior and peritoneal prolapse. Protect the table with absorbent pads; privacy matters for a real evacuation.

Sequences

Static T2-weighted FSE in three planes at rest for anatomy and the levator ani. Dynamic midsagittal imaging with single-shot FSE or balanced SSFP at roughly one image per second: rest, squeeze (Kegel), strain (Valsalva), then repeated evacuation until the rectum is empty, then post-evacuation strain. bSSFP gives high temporal resolution with dark fat–water boundaries (India-ink artifact); single-shot FSE is less sensitive to susceptibility but slightly blurrier.

Position and pitfalls

Supine closed-bore imaging is standard and adequate if evacuation is achieved; upright open-bore imaging is more physiologic. Pitfalls: poor strain effort, no evacuation phase (intussusception and enteroceles often appear only at the end of evacuation), no rectal gel, an off-midline slice that underestimates descent, and measuring on a frame that is not the worst one.

Teaching simulation. The pelvis is a schematic, synthetic midsagittal model with simplified organ motion, contrast and noise; distances, angles and thresholds are representative and simplified. Grading thresholds and even landmark definitions vary between publications (for example, the coccygeal end of the PCL is the last coccygeal joint in the ACR–SAR–SPR template but the first–second coccygeal junction in the HMO illustration of Boyadzhyan et al.); follow your local template. Anorectal angle values in particular vary widely between studies, so this lesson emphasizes their change between phases rather than absolute numbers. For teaching only; not for clinical use.
